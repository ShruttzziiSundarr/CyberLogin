Authentication vs. Authorization

  

    

      
Authentication · AuthN

      
Prove who you are

      
The system challenges you to demonstrate an identity claim. You respond with a factor — something you know, have, or are. If the answer matches, the system now has a verified principal.

      
Hotel analogy: showing your passport at check-in. The clerk decides your claim "I am Alice Smith" is credible.

    

    

      
Authorization · AuthZ

      
Decide what you can do

      
After identity is established, policy decides which resources the principal may access and what operations they may perform. Two users can authenticate to the same system and get completely different capabilities.

      
Hotel analogy: the key card the clerk hands you. It opens your room and the gym, not the kitchen or the penthouse.

    

  

  

    
Why it matters

    
Confusing the two is how bugs happen. A system that authenticates every request but applies no authorization is a locked front door with no interior walls. Conversely, rich authorization policies are useless if the identity claims feeding them can be spoofed. They are different layers, with different owners, different failure modes, and different audit trails.

  


  
Privileged Access Management · PAM

  

    

      

        

          
          

            
PAM is the set of controls around accounts that can break things: root, Domain Admin, service accounts, shared credentials for network devices. These accounts are both the highest value and the least protected targets in most environments.

            
A PAM solution typically vaults passwords, enforces session recording, and brokers access so the human never sees the credential at all — they request access, get a time-limited session, and the password rotates when the session closes.

          

        

        

          
          

            
Standing access means the account exists and has permissions permanently, even between uses. An admin account sitting idle for eleven months still has Domain Admin rights. When it gets compromised, the attacker inherits eleven months of unchecked privilege instantly.

            
Just-in-time (JIT) access means the account is created or elevated only for the duration of an approved task. The privilege disappears when the window closes. This shrinks the blast radius of credential theft to the length of a single session.

          

        

        

          
          

            
When humans cannot see the password, session recording becomes the audit layer. Every keystroke, every command, every file accessed in a privileged session is captured and tied to the identity of the person who checked out the session — not to the shared account they used.

            
This is what lets a forensics team reconstruct exactly what a compromised account did, and what lets HR conversations happen with evidence rather than conjecture.

          

        

        

          
          

            
Service accounts are the forgotten corner of PAM. They often have high privileges, rarely rotate passwords because the teams wired them into eight different systems without documenting where, and never hit MFA because they are non-interactive.

            
Modern mitigation: managed identities (workload identity), short-lived tokens from a secrets manager, or certificate-based authentication rather than passwords. If a service account has a password that has never changed, that is the first question in any incident review.

          

        

      

    

    

      
The PAM request flow

      

        

          

            
1

            

              Request
              
Engineer submits a request for privileged access — specific system, specific time window, business justification. Human or policy approval gate.

            

          

          

            
2

            

              Checkout
              
PAM vault issues a session token or a rotated credential. The engineer never sees the underlying password. The checkout is logged with their identity.

            

          

          

            
3

            

              Session (recorded)
              
All activity in the privileged session is captured. The session is time-boxed. An alert fires if the window is exceeded.

            

          

          

            
4

            

              Rotation
              
On session close the credential rotates automatically. The leaked credential from this session is immediately invalid.

Identity Governance and Administration · IGA

  

    

      
The joiner-mover-leaver lifecycle

      

        

          

            
J

            

              Joiner
              
New employee. IGA provisions accounts, assigns role-based entitlements from an approved template, and sends access confirmations. SCIM carries the provisioning instruction to each downstream system.

            

          

          

            
M

            

              Mover
              
Employee changes role or team. IGA should remove old entitlements and add new ones. In practice this is where privilege creep begins — the old access is often never removed.

            

          

          

            
L

            

              Leaver
              
Employee exits. IGA must disable or delete accounts across all systems within a defined SLA — usually same-day. An account that stays active after offboarding is a standing vulnerability.

            

          

        

      

    

    

      
Access reviews and recertification

      

        

          

            
            

              
A periodic (quarterly, annual) process in which managers review and confirm or revoke their team members' entitlements. The goal is to catch access that accumulated without a corresponding business need, and to demonstrate to auditors that someone with accountability signed off on what exists.

            

          

          

            
            

              
Over time, movers accumulate entitlements from every role they have ever held. Nobody removes old access because it is not actively causing harm, and the mover needs it briefly during transitions. After five years, a treasury analyst who spent six months in IT helpdesk still has local admin on every laptop they ever touched.

              
This is the most common finding in access audits and the least dramatic-looking vulnerability, right up until a breach uses it to pivot laterally across eight systems with one set of credentials.

            

          

          

            
            

              
Every principal — user, service, or process — should hold only the minimum permissions required to do its job, for only as long as it is doing that job.

              
In practice this means: deny by default, grant explicitly, scope tightly, expire aggressively. The principle is easy to state and expensive to maintain because it conflicts with "just give them access so we can ship."

            

          

        

      

    

  


  
Cookies in Browser Auth

  

    
What a session cookie is doing

    

      
Both the SAML and OIDC flows in tab 00 end the same way: the SP/RP mints a session cookie and hands it to the browser. Every subsequent request the browser makes to that domain carries the cookie automatically — that is the HTTP mechanism that turns a one-time authentication event into a sustained session.

      
The attributes on that cookie decide its security posture:
        

          HttpOnly — JS cannot read it
          Secure — only sent over TLS
          SameSite=Strict/Lax — CSRF protection
          SameSite=None — required for cross-origin but needs Secure
          missing HttpOnly — XSS can steal it
          missing Secure — exposed over HTTP
        

      

      
The SAML assertion and the OIDC tokens are not the session. They are the mechanism that earns the right to create a session. Once the cookie exists, the tokens are useless and should be discarded. Keeping them in browser storage after the session is established is a mistake with no upside.

    

  









  
Kerberos

  

    

      
The three actors

      

        

          

            👤
            
Client

            
The workstation or service requesting access. Knows its own long-term key (derived from the user's password).

          

          

            🔑
            
KDC (Key Distribution Center)

            
Combines the Authentication Server (AS) and the Ticket Granting Server (TGS). Knows the keys of every principal.

          

          

            📄
            
Service

            
The resource being accessed. Validates the service ticket but never contacts the KDC during authentication.

          

        

        

          

            
1

            

              AS-REQ → KDC
              
Client asks the Authentication Server for a Ticket Granting Ticket (TGT). Proves it knows its key without transmitting it.

            

          

          

            
2

            

              AS-REP ← KDC
              
KDC returns the TGT (encrypted with the KDC's own key, unreadable to the client) plus a session key encrypted with the client's key.

            

          

          

            
3

            

              TGS-REQ → KDC
              
Client presents the TGT to the Ticket Granting Server and requests a service ticket for a specific target service.

            

          

          

            
4

            

              TGS-REP ← KDC
              
KDC issues a service ticket (encrypted with the service's key) and a new session key for the client-service conversation.

            

          

          

            
5

            

              AP-REQ → Service
              
Client presents the service ticket. The service decrypts it with its own key, verifies the authenticator, and the session begins. The KDC is never contacted again.

            

          

        

      

    

    

      
Why Kerberos matters today

      

        

          

            
            

              
Every time you log into a Windows machine joined to Active Directory, Kerberos handles the authentication. Every file share, every printer, every SQL Server connection is using service tickets. It is the infrastructure underneath most enterprise environments, even ones that have added SAML and OIDC on top.

            

          

          

            
            

              
Pass-the-Ticket: steal a valid TGT or service ticket from memory (e.g., with Mimikatz) and replay it from a different machine. The KDC cannot tell the difference.

              
Golden Ticket: compromise the KRBTGT account key and forge TGTs for any principal, with any group membership, with any expiry. The only mitigation is rotating the KRBTGT key twice (to invalidate cached tickets), which disrupts the domain.

            

          

          

            
            

              
Any authenticated domain user can request a service ticket for any service account with an SPN (Service Principal Name). The ticket is encrypted with the service account's password hash. Take the ticket offline and crack the hash at leisure. Service accounts with weak passwords are fully exposed to every domain user.

              
Mitigation: long random passwords on service accounts, managed service accounts (gMSA), and monitoring for unusual TGS-REQ patterns.

            

          

        

      

    

  


  
SCIM · System for Cross-domain Identity Management

  

    

      
What SCIM automates

      

        
Before SCIM, provisioning a new employee meant logging into each SaaS application and manually creating an account. Fifteen apps meant fifteen manual steps, plus fifteen more on offboarding — except offboarding usually got skipped because it was painful.

        
SCIM (RFC 7643/7644) is a REST API standard that lets an identity provider push account lifecycle events to any compliant system. The IdP sends an HTTP POST to create a user, a PATCH to update attributes or group membership, and a DELETE to remove them. The downstream system handles the rest.

        

          POST /Users — create account
          PATCH /Users/{id} — update attributes
          PUT /Users/{id} — full replacement
          DELETE /Users/{id} — deprovision
        

      

    

    

      
SCIM vs. SSO

      

        
These solve different problems and are often deployed together. SSO (via SAML or OIDC) handles the moment of authentication — it logs a user in without a password. SCIM handles account existence — it creates, updates, and removes the account so there is something to log into.

        
Without SCIM you can have SSO: user authenticates at the IdP, the SP receives an assertion for an account it has never seen, and depending on the SP either JIT provisions a bare-bones account or rejects the login entirely. SCIM makes the account exist before the user first logs in, with correct attributes and group memberships already in place.

        
Without SSO you can have SCIM: accounts exist everywhere but each app has its own login page. The combination of both is the baseline for modern enterprise identity.

      

    

  


  
RADIUS · Remote Authentication Dial-In User Service

  

    

      
The AAA model

      

        

          

            
A

            

              Authentication
              
Who is this? User presents credentials to the NAS (Network Access Server — a VPN gateway, Wi-Fi controller, switch). NAS forwards them to the RADIUS server.

            

          

          

            
A

            

              Authorization
              
What can they do? RADIUS Access-Accept response carries attributes: VLAN assignment, IP pool, session limits, permitted services. The NAS enforces whatever the RADIUS server sends back.

            

          

          

            
A

            

              Accounting
              
What did they do? Start/stop records logged by the RADIUS server: bytes transferred, session duration, device MAC. The audit trail for network access.

            

          

        

      

    

    

      
Where RADIUS lives

      

        

          

            
            

              
When an endpoint plugs into a managed switch or connects to corporate Wi-Fi, 802.1X requires it to authenticate before the port is opened. The switch acts as the NAS, the endpoint runs a supplicant, and RADIUS is the back-end decision engine. An unauthenticated device stays in a quarantine VLAN with no access to internal resources.

            

          

          

            
            

              
RADIUS is a 1991 protocol that uses UDP, a shared secret between NAS and server, and MD5-based message authentication — not impressive by modern standards. TACACS+ is a Cisco alternative that separates the three A functions and uses TCP with full packet encryption.

              
RADIUS is not going away because network infrastructure has extremely long replacement cycles and almost every managed switch, VPN appliance, and wireless controller speaks it natively. Understanding RADIUS means understanding why every enterprise network access policy still routes through it.

            

          

        

      

    

  


  
OIDC Endpoints

  

    
What lives at each endpoint — Microsoft Entra ID / PingFederate

    

      

        
          

            Endpoint
            Who calls it
            What it returns / does
          

        
        
          

            /.well-known/openid-configuration
            Client at startup
            Discovery document. JSON blob listing all other endpoints, supported scopes, algorithms, and JWKS URI. Never hardcode endpoints — fetch discovery once and cache it.
          

          

            /authorize
            Browser (user-agent)
            The authorization endpoint. User authenticates here, Conditional Access / MFA applies, session is evaluated. Returns an authorization code to the redirect URI.
          

          

            /token
            Backend server (back channel)
            Exchanges a code (+ code_verifier for PKCE) for tokens. Returns id_token, access_token, and optionally refresh_token as JSON. The browser never touches this endpoint.
          

          

            /introspect
            Resource server (API)
            Lets a resource server validate an opaque access token it received without having to decode a JWT. Returns active: true/false plus claims. Requires client auth. Add rate limiting — an attacker can probe validity of stolen tokens.
          

          

            /jwks
            Client verifying a JWT
            JSON Web Key Set. The public keys the IdP uses to sign JWTs. Clients fetch this (keyed by kid), cache aggressively, and only refetch on unknown kid. Never disable caching — JWKS fetching is a DoS vector.
          

          

            /logout / end_session
            Browser (RP-initiated)
            Clears the IdP session and optionally propagates logout to all RPs via back-channel logout or front-channel iframes. The RP must also clear its own session cookie independently.
          

        
      

    

  


  

    
PingFederate vs. Microsoft Entra ID

    
Both implement OIDC and SAML and act as identity providers. PingFederate is an on-premises (or cloud-hosted) federation server that organizations deploy and manage themselves — it is the integration layer when you need to bridge legacy enterprise directories, RADIUS, LDAP, and modern apps. Microsoft Entra ID (formerly Azure AD) is a cloud-native IdP where the infrastructure is Microsoft's problem. The OIDC endpoints above exist on both. The operational difference is who owns the certificate rotation, the uptime, and the Conditional Access policy engine.

  









  
OAuth 2.0 Roles

  

    

      
Resource Owner

      
The user

      
Owns the data being accessed. Grants permission to the client to act on their behalf. The resource owner is not always present — client credentials flows have no resource owner at all.

    

    

      
Client

      
The application

      
Requests access on behalf of the resource owner. Can be a browser app (public client, no secret) or a server-side app (confidential client, has a secret).

    

  

  

    

      
Authorization Server

      
The IdP

      
Authenticates the resource owner, gets consent, and issues tokens. Exposes the /authorize and /token endpoints. In OIDC this is also called the OpenID Provider (OP).

    

    

      
Resource Server

      
The API

      
Hosts the protected resources. Validates the access token on every request. The access token tells it who the caller is and what they are allowed to do.

Why the implicit grant is dead

      

        
The original OAuth spec included an implicit grant for browser apps that could not keep secrets. The authorization server returned the access token directly in the redirect URL fragment. It worked. It also put a live token in the browser history, in referrer headers, and in any server log that captured the redirect URL.

        
The authorization code flow with PKCE solves this without needing a client secret. PKCE is the reason the implicit grant is deprecated and should not appear in new code. If you see it in a codebase today, it is a migration todo.

PKCE deep dive · code verifier and code challenge

      

        

          

            
1

            

              Generate code_verifier
              
The client generates a cryptographically random 43–128 character string. This never leaves the client. Store it in the session.

            

          

          

            
2

            

              Derive code_challenge
              
code_challenge = BASE64URL(SHA256(code_verifier)) — method S256. The challenge is the only thing the authorization server sees at this point.

            

          

          

            
3

            

              Authorization request
              
Client redirects the browser to /authorize with code_challenge and code_challenge_method=S256. User authenticates. Server stores the challenge against the code it is about to issue.

            

          

          

            
4

            

              Authorization code arrives
              
Browser receives a short-lived, single-use code at the redirect URI. The code is useless alone. An attacker who intercepts it cannot use it without the verifier.

            

          

          

            
5

            

              Token exchange (back channel)
              
Server POSTs the code and the code_verifier to /token. The IdP recomputes SHA256(verifier) and compares it to the stored challenge. Match = tokens issued. No match = rejected. The verifier is the proof of origin.

            

          

        

      

    

    

      
State vs. Nonce — the confusion that never ends

      

        
state is an opaque value the client sends in the authorization request and receives back at the redirect. Its job is CSRF protection on the redirect: it proves the callback came from the authorization server that answered your specific request, not from an attacker who crafted a redirect.

        
nonce is embedded in the ID token itself (by the authorization server) and checked by the client after the token arrives. Its job is replay protection on the token: it proves the token was issued in response to your specific authentication request, not replayed from an earlier one.

        
They operate at different layers. State protects the redirect. Nonce protects the token. You need both, and having one does not give you the other.

        

          state → on the redirect URI
          nonce → inside the id_token
          both are checked by the client
        

      

    

  


  
Other grant types

  

    

      

        
        

          
No user. The client authenticates as itself — with a client_id and client_secret, or with a certificate — and receives an access token scoped to its own permissions. Used for microservices, cron jobs, and any backend process that needs to call a protected API without a user in the loop.

          
The critical security difference: these tokens represent a service identity, not a user. Compromise of the client secret is the equivalent of credential theft. Rotate secrets, use short-lived certificates where possible, and scope to the minimum required permissions. This is the grant type most commonly used in systems that "didn't think they needed OAuth" until they needed to call a protected API.

          
no user involvementconfidential client onlyno refresh token issued

        

      

      

        
        

          
The device cannot open a browser. It calls /device_authorization and receives a device_code, a user_code, and a verification_uri. It displays the user_code and URI to the human (e.g., on a TV screen). The human goes to that URI on a phone or laptop, enters the code, and authenticates. The device polls /token until the authorization server confirms the human completed the flow, then receives tokens.

          
The security property: the browser-based authentication still happens on a real browser, on a device the user controls. The low-capability device only ever sees the token, never the credentials. The user_code expires quickly; polling has a rate limit enforced by slow_down responses.

          
user authenticates on a separate devicepolling requireddevice_code is short-lived

        

      

      

        
        

          
Access tokens are short-lived by design — typically one hour. When one expires, the client can use a refresh token (obtained alongside the original access token) to get a new access token without user interaction. The refresh token is long-lived and must be stored securely.

          
Most IdPs implement refresh token rotation: each use of the refresh token returns a new refresh token and invalidates the old one. A reuse of an already-used refresh token triggers a full token family revocation — a signal that the token was likely stolen, so every session using that lineage is killed. If a legitimate client loses its refresh token in a race condition and triggers this, the user just has to log in again. That is an acceptable trade-off.

JWT structure — what you are actually validating

    
A JWT is three base64url-encoded sections separated by dots: header.payload.signature. The header names the algorithm and key id. The payload carries the claims. The signature is the cryptographic proof that an entity with the private key produced exactly this header and payload. The claim that trips people: the signature proves integrity but not meaning. An attacker cannot modify a valid JWT and keep the signature valid — but they can present a valid JWT that was issued for a different audience, a different purpose, or issued minutes after the last actual user interaction. Signature valid ≠ safe to trust. The claim validation after the signature check is where the actual security lives.
